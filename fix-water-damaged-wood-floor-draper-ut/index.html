How to Fix a Water Damaged Wood Floor in Draper, UT

Whether a wood floor can be dried in place or has to be cut out depends on what it is made of, how long it stayed wet and what is underneath it. This guide walks through the first hour, the warning signs and a decision table, and links to our water damage repair service for the rebuild.

By the Draper Water Damage Pros team

The first hour matters more than the fix

A dishwasher supply line seeps overnight, and by morning the kitchen floor feels soft near the sink. What you do in the next hour sets up everything else. Quick action often decides whether a floor needs drying or replacing.

  1. Shut off the water source and, if the area is near outlets or appliances, turn off power to that circuit.
  2. Mop or vacuum up standing water. A wet-dry vacuum works well on plank floors.
  3. Pull rugs, mats and furniture legs off the wood, and lift anything that was sitting on it.
  4. Run fans and, if you have one, a dehumidifier. Keep windows closed so the dehumidifier is not fighting outside air.
  5. Photograph the floor from several angles before anything moves, and note the time you found the water.

If the water covered a large area or came from a source you cannot stop, our emergency water removal crew can get extraction equipment in. EPA advises drying water-damaged areas within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth, so speed counts.

Reading the warning signs: cupping, crowning and buckling

Boards react to moisture in recognizable ways. Knowing which one you have tells you how far along the damage is.

Cupping

The edges of each board rise above the center, so the surface looks like a row of shallow troughs. It usually means the underside absorbed more moisture than the top, and it often eases once the floor dries slowly and evenly.

Crowning

The center of each board rises above the edges. It can appear after a cupped floor was sanded before it was fully dry, or when moisture comes from above and the surface swells.

Buckling

Boards lift away from the subfloor, sometimes in a tent shape. This is the most serious sign, because the flooring has pulled off its fastening and is rarely worth saving once it has.

Decision table: dry in place or take it out

The right answer depends heavily on flooring type. Use this table as a starting point, then confirm with moisture readings in the boards and the subfloor.

Flooring typeUsually dried in place whenUsually removed when
Solid hardwoodWater was clean, caught fast, and boards are only slightly cuppedBoards have buckled, black staining appears or water sat for days
Engineered woodOnly a thin spill reached the surface and the seams stayed tightLayers have separated, edges swell or the core feels soft
LaminateRarely. Fiberboard cores absorb water and rarely recoverNearly always once seams swell or planks lift
Subfloor under any of theseReadings come back to normal and there is no soft spotPanels are swollen, delaminated or smell musty

Solid hardwood is the most forgiving, which is why it is often dried in place when the water was clean. Laminate is the least forgiving, because the compressed fiber core swells permanently.

Water that came from a sewer backup or a dirty source changes the table completely. Contaminated water usually means porous materials come out, regardless of flooring type.

What drying in place actually involves

Drying a floor in place is not just pointing a fan at it. Crews often use panels or mats that pull air through the boards, along with dehumidifiers that keep room humidity low enough for the wood to release moisture.

The key is slow, steady drying. Rushing it with heat can cause boards to split or cup harder, so a careful crew tracks readings daily and adjusts. If you want to see how that process works across a whole house, our water mitigation page lays out the equipment and logging.

Expect the floor to move even after it feels dry. Boards can keep shifting for days or weeks as moisture balances out, which is why sanding or refinishing should wait until a meter says the wood and subfloor are ready.

When boards or the subfloor have to come out

Sometimes the surface is not the main problem. Say a water heater leaks onto a ground-floor room and the water runs under the baseboards into the subfloor. The boards may look recoverable, but a plywood subfloor that stays wet will swell, loosen the nails and hold moisture against the underside of the wood.

Signs that point to removal include:

  • Boards that feel spongy or compress when you press them
  • A musty odor that persists after the room has been dried
  • Dark staining that runs along seams or nail lines
  • Subfloor panels that look swollen at the edges or have delaminated layers
  • Fastener heads that have started to rust

Pulling a few rows is often enough to expose the subfloor and let it dry. A repair crew can then reinstall or replace boards, and a fresh finish can be blended into the rest of the room.

What to do before anyone arrives

Do

Document damage, move furniture, run a dehumidifier and keep foot traffic off buckled boards.

Do not

Do not sand, seal or cover a wet floor. Trapped moisture can ruin boards that might have recovered.

Do

Check your policy. Sudden and accidental water damage is often covered, while gradual leaks usually are not, so ask your insurer.

Do not

Do not use heat guns or space heaters aimed at the boards. Uneven heat can split planks.

Standard to ask about

The published ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for water damage restoration is the reference many professionals work from, and you can ask any company whether its crews follow it. For wood floors, the useful question is whether the crew will take moisture readings each day and show you the numbers before deciding to pull boards.

Common questions

Will cupped floorboards flatten on their own?

Often they ease as the floor dries slowly and the moisture balances out, though it can take weeks. Do not sand until a meter shows the wood is dry. If the boards stay cupped after they test dry, a refinish or board replacement may be needed.

Is engineered wood easier to save than solid hardwood?

Not usually. Engineered planks have layers glued together, and water can separate them or swell the edges. Thin spills that are cleaned up fast can be fine, but soaked engineered flooring is commonly replaced.

Can I leave laminate in place if it dried out?

Laminate with a fiber core that swelled rarely returns to its original shape. Planks may look fine at first, then show raised seams later. If seams have lifted, replacement is the usual outcome.

Does the April irrigation startup in Draper affect my floors?

It can, indirectly. When secondary water comes on, a damaged line near a foundation can leak unnoticed into a slab or crawl space. Check baseboards and ground-level floors for dampness in the first week after startup.
