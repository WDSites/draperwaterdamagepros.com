Does Home Insurance Cover Water Damage in Draper, UT?

Sometimes yes, sometimes no, and the difference usually comes down to how the water arrived and what your specific policy says. This guide explains the common patterns and how to document a loss, and it does not promise coverage for any claim, because only your policy and your insurer decide that.

By the Draper Water Damage Pros team

The question insurers ask first: sudden or gradual?

Standard homeowners policies tend to draw a line between water damage that is sudden and accidental and damage that happens slowly. A supply line that bursts overnight is the first kind. A pipe that has dripped inside a wall for months is the second.

Sudden and accidental water damage is often covered by homeowners policies. Gradual leaks, seepage, and damage from neglect often are not. The exact wording varies by policy, so the only reliable answer comes from reading yours and asking your insurer.

Many policies also expect you to protect the home from further damage once a loss happens. That is one reason prompt water damage cleanup matters, and why the damaged area should be dried quickly rather than left while you wait for a call back.

Flood is a separate policy, and the waiting period matters

The Utah Insurance Department states that standard homeowners and renters policies do not cover flood losses, including post-wildfire mudflows. Flood insurance has to be bought separately, either through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private insurer. For any rising-water loss, a house with only a standard policy has a gap.

A few details from the department's flood FAQ are worth knowing before you need them:

  • The standard NFIP waiting period is 30 days, so a policy bought during a storm will not help that storm.
  • Flood insurance does not cover damage from internal water sources such as a burst pipe, which belongs to your homeowners policy.
  • NFIP covers a sewer or drain backup only if an active surface flood directly caused it.
  • NFIP basement coverage is limited. It includes items like furnaces, water heaters, and electrical boxes, and does not cover finished drywall, paint, flooring, built-in cabinets, or personal items.

If the water came from outside, see flood damage restoration for what the response looks like.

Sewer and drain backup: ask about the endorsement

A backup is one of the most common surprises. Water coming up through a floor drain or basement toilet is often excluded from a standard policy, and some policies add coverage through an endorsement.

Ask whether your policy has a water backup endorsement, and if so, what it covers and what the limit is. Ask the same question about sump pump failure, since that can be listed differently. Do it before a loss, when there is time to compare.

Documenting the loss before anything moves

Good records make the claim conversation simpler, whatever the outcome. Start the minute the water is stopped and everyone is safe.

1

Photograph everything

Wide shots of each room, then close-ups of the damage, the source, and any water line on walls. Include the ceiling, floor, and contents.

2

Record a walkthrough video

Walk slowly through the affected rooms and describe what you see out loud. Include the date.

3

List damaged items

Write down what each item was, roughly when it was bought, and its condition. Note serial numbers on electronics and appliances.

4

Keep every receipt

Hold on to bills for emergency drying, temporary housing, hotel stays, and replacement supplies. Keep drying logs and moisture readings from the crew.

Do not discard damaged items too early

Keep damaged materials and belongings so the adjuster can see them, unless they are a health risk, such as sewage-soaked items or visibly moldy porous materials. If you must remove something, photograph it first and keep a sample.

Questions to ask your insurer on the first call

  • Is this type of water damage covered under my policy, and what is my deductible?
  • What mitigation steps do you expect me to take now, and can I begin drying before the adjuster visits?
  • Does my policy have a water backup endorsement or a flood policy?
  • How do I submit photos, receipts, and the drying documentation?
  • Does the policy cover temporary living costs if the home cannot be used?
  • Is there a time limit for filing the claim?

Write down the name of the person you speak with, the date, and what they said. Ask for anything important in writing.

Where a restoration company fits in

A crew stops the loss from getting larger by removing standing water, setting air movers and LGR dehumidifiers, and logging moisture readings so the drying is documented. That is the reasonable-steps part of a claim, whatever the final decision on coverage. For a burst pipe, start with emergency water removal.

The crew can share its logs and photos with you and your insurer. It cannot tell you what will be covered, because that decision is the insurer's alone. Homeowners in South Jordan or Herriman can call the same number to get drying started while the claim conversation happens.

Draper note: groundwater, mudflows, and flood coverage

In January 2023, more than thirty homes in Draper's Bellevue neighborhood were hit by groundwater flooding in a private system, and several had inches of water inside. The city said its storm drain system was operating without issue. That mix of causes is exactly why you should ask your insurer how groundwater, seepage, and a private system are treated before you assume anything is covered.

The Corner Canyon fire of 2008 and a mud flood in 2009 near 12600 South 2100 East are a reminder that burn scars can send mud downhill after heavy rain. The Utah Insurance Department notes that standard homeowners policies exclude post-wildfire mudflows, and the NFIP waiting period is 30 days. If your home sits below a hillside, ask about flood coverage when the weather is quiet.

Common questions

Does homeowners insurance cover a burst pipe?

Often yes, since a sudden burst is the classic sudden and accidental event. What is covered, and the deductible, depends on your policy. Call your insurer, then get the water stopped and drying started.

Is a slow leak covered?

Often not. Many policies exclude gradual leakage or damage from long-term neglect, though some cover the resulting damage. Ask how your policy words it.

Does my policy cover a sewer backup?

Often only with an add-on. Ask whether your policy has a water backup endorsement and what the limit is. Without one, a backup may be excluded.

Should I start cleanup before the adjuster arrives?

You can usually begin emergency drying to prevent more damage, but ask your insurer first. Take photos and video before moving anything, and keep damaged items for the adjuster unless they are a health risk.

I live below the hillside in Draper. Does my regular policy cover mud?

The Utah Insurance Department says standard homeowners and renters policies do not cover flood losses, including post-wildfire mudflows. That requires a separate flood policy, and the standard waiting period is 30 days. Talk to your agent about options before the next storm.
